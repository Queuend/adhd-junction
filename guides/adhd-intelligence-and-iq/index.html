ADHD and intelligence: can you have ADHD and a high IQ?

A plain-English synthesis of research on ADHD, high intelligence, giftedness and cognitive performance.

ADHD Junction Research Guide. Last research check September 2026.

Being intelligent does not rule out ADHD.

It may sound obvious when put as simply as that, but intelligence can sometimes complicate the picture. A child who performs exceptionally well at school may not fit people's expectations of ADHD. An adult with an impressive academic record or a demanding career may wonder how they could possibly have reached that point if they had always had difficulties with attention or organisation.

Research suggests that the answer is more complicated than simply comparing ADHD with a person's IQ.

ADHD is not a measure of intelligence

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition involving persistent patterns of inattention and/or hyperactivity and impulsivity that interfere with everyday functioning.

An intelligence test measures something quite different. Depending on the test being used, it may examine areas such as verbal comprehension, reasoning, working memory and processing speed.

This means that an ADHD assessment and an intelligence test are not different ways of measuring the same thing.

Someone can therefore have ADHD at any level of intellectual ability, including a high one.

Research involving gifted children supports this distinction. Gomez and colleagues compared children with ADHD, gifted children, children who were both gifted and had ADHD, and a clinical control group. Their findings supported ADHD as a valid diagnosis in gifted children rather than suggesting that high intelligence somehow excluded it.

Interestingly, the gifted children with ADHD showed somewhat lower levels of inattention than the non-gifted ADHD group, while their levels of hyperactivity and impulsivity were comparable.

That is an important distinction. Intelligence may influence how some ADHD difficulties appear without making ADHD disappear.

Can intelligence hide ADHD?

This is where things become particularly interesting.

A person with high cognitive ability may have strengths that help them deal with some of their difficulties. A child might understand new material very quickly, for example, despite frequently losing concentration during a lesson. Someone may perform extremely well in examinations despite having considerable difficulty organising their work beforehand.

That does not necessarily mean intelligence is "masking" ADHD in every such case. Nor should success at school or work itself be treated as evidence of an underlying condition.

It does show why achievement alone cannot tell us whether somebody has ADHD.

A clinical assessment looks much more widely at the person's history, their difficulties in everyday life, the settings in which these occur and the extent to which they cause impairment.

What does research tell us about ADHD and cognitive performance?

Research looking at ADHD and cognitive performance has found associations at a group level between ADHD and measures including IQ, working memory, reaction time and other aspects of cognitive functioning.

Those group differences need to be interpreted carefully.

An average difference between two groups does not tell us what the intelligence of an individual person will be. People with ADHD vary enormously, just as people without ADHD do.

A longitudinal study by Vainieri and colleagues followed people from childhood and adolescence into young adulthood and examined IQ, short-term memory, working memory and reaction-time measures.

The researchers found relationships between ADHD and several aspects of cognitive performance over time. ADHD earlier in life also predicted lower working-memory performance at follow-up.

This does not mean that ADHD determines a person's intelligence, or that somebody's IQ can be used to decide whether they have ADHD. It instead demonstrates that the relationship between ADHD and cognitive performance is complex and can develop over time.

What about very high intelligence or giftedness?

This has received particular attention because giftedness can sometimes complicate recognition of ADHD.

There are really two potential problems.

The first is assuming that a highly intelligent child cannot have ADHD because they are doing too well academically.

The second is going in the opposite direction and assuming that behaviours associated with giftedness must be evidence of ADHD.

Neither assumption is safe.

Research comparing gifted children with ADHD, gifted children without ADHD and non-gifted children with ADHD has found differences that can help distinguish the groups. One study by Gauthier and colleagues, for example, examined how parents and teachers rated the behaviour of children in these different groups.

The results reinforce the importance of looking at the child's overall pattern of behaviour and functioning rather than treating giftedness or a high IQ score as a shortcut either towards or away from an ADHD diagnosis.

New research makes the picture even more interesting

A large study published in 2026 examined 5,138 children aged 6 to 16 who had ADHD, using behavioural assessments, cognitive testing and brain-imaging data.

Rather than finding a simple relationship in which increasing intelligence was uniformly associated with fewer ADHD characteristics, the researchers found a more complicated pattern.

Higher verbal IQ was associated with lower levels of inattention in the study, but also with higher impulsivity scores. Relationships involving performance IQ also differed with age.

The researchers described intelligence as potentially having different relationships with different aspects of ADHD rather than acting as one straightforward protective factor.

This is one study, and its findings should not be turned into rules for individual people. It does, however, provide another reason to be cautious about statements such as "someone that intelligent couldn't have ADHD."

“But I did well at school”

Doing well at school does not establish whether somebody has ADHD.

Neither does doing badly.

Two pupils could achieve similar grades while getting there in completely different ways. One may work steadily and keep on top of deadlines. Another might repeatedly postpone work, forget what needs doing and then rely on intense last-minute effort.

Equally, those behaviours themselves are not proof of ADHD. Many things can affect concentration, motivation, memory, organisation and academic performance.

For an ADHD assessment, what matters is the broader and persistent pattern: what difficulties are present, when they began, where they occur, how much they interfere with everyday life, and whether something else provides a better explanation.

Intelligence and executive functioning are not the same thing

This distinction can help explain some of the apparent contradiction.

A person can be very capable at reasoning, understanding complicated ideas or acquiring knowledge while still finding particular aspects of everyday self-management difficult.

Executive functions include processes involved in planning, organising, holding information in mind, regulating behaviour and shifting attention between tasks.

They are related to cognitive ability, but they are not simply another name for intelligence.

This is one reason somebody can be intellectually capable while still struggling considerably with activities that appear, from the outside, as though they ought to be easy.

So does a high IQ make ADHD less likely?

A high IQ should not be used on its own either to rule ADHD out or to establish that it is present.

Research suggests that intellectual ability can interact with the way ADHD characteristics are expressed. Some difficulties may be less obvious in certain people, while others remain significant.

The more useful question is therefore not:

“Is this person too intelligent to have ADHD?”

It is:

“Is there a persistent pattern of ADHD characteristics that causes meaningful difficulties in this person's life?”

That is a question about the whole person, not their IQ score.
